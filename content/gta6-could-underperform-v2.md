title: "GTA 6 Could Underperform Even If It Sells Tens of Millions"
date: 2026-11-10
author: "L. K."
description: "A deep dive into upcoming releases."
---

GTA 6 is not necessarily headed for a bomb, but it could significantly underperform, even with the tens of millions of copies the game is expected to sell. Its massive production and marketing budgets mean it could still miss financial expectations without technically being a flop.

Now, nobody outside Rockstar knows for certain what the game cost, but some analysts, including at Business Insider, put the figure at roughly $1 billion to $1.5 billion, though that came from anonymous sources rather than company documents. For comparison, GTA V cost about $265 million, development and marketing combined, so the new game's price tag may be several times larger.

A budget that size changes the definition of success. Analysts assume the game will print money, but the math is tighter than it looks.

After platforms and retailers take their cut, one estimate says day-one sales only roughly cover the costs, and chatter around the industry holds that 20 million copies on day one is the minimum needed to avoid disappointment.

When the bar sits that high, a huge launch can still read as a miss, and the road to launch has done little to lower expectations. The game has been delayed twice, landing on November 19, 2026, after slipping from 2025 to May and then to November, and every delay wears down a little goodwill.

The rollout has added to that wear. A rumored May pre-order date turned out to be a fake-out that annoyed fans, and when pre-orders finally opened in June, no gameplay footage was public, yet the game was already priced at $80.
Asking for money before showing the game is a big ask, even from Rockstar, and the price itself compounds the problem. The standard edition costs $79.99 and the Ultimate Edition $99.99, and pre-orders arrived with no physical disc, only a code in a box, which sparked a heated debate about owning games.
Some fans suspected the move was aimed at shutting down the used-game market.

Combined with the title’s current console exclusivity, which forces interested players to absorb the cost of new hardware alongside the game itself, it becomes easy to see why casual buyers might hold off until prices drop. That hesitation poses a real risk to publishers, as those casual gamers are precisely the demographic that optimistic day-one sales forecasts rely on.

Culture wars have also become a genuine commercial force in gaming, and dismissing them as background noise underestimates the risk. For instance, Insomniac Games' Marvel’s Wolverine faced a relentless backlash across social media, ultimately contributing to its commercial underperformance relative to the studio's hit Spider-Man series.

Gears of War: E-Day went through something similar in October, with critics pinning the "woke" label on the character Mags Carter and the game's LGBTQ+ customization options. The game massively underperformed, and Bioware, which produced E-day, is shutting down.

GTA 6, which has a dual-protagonist setup is also fueling anti-woke discourse, with internet commentators labelling female lead Lucia a "girlboss" designed to overshadow her partner Jason.

On a launch where commercial expectations are astronomical, even pre-release cultural drag represents a genuine business risk.

However, the deepest risk may be the Rockstar's business model itself. Take-Two, Rockstar's parent company, earns most of its money from players who keep spending after a purchase, with $5.20 billion of last fiscal year's $6.66 billion in revenue coming from that kind of recurring spending. Yet the CEO told shareholders that no recurring spending is expected with this launch, because the game ships as single-player only, and a Bloomberg reporter says the company has no plans to discuss the new online mode anytime soon. At launch, then, the company's most profitable trick is missing, and everything rides on one-time sales.

That gap creates a tricky balancing act. Take-Two says it will keep supporting the current GTA Online and putting serious resources into it, so Rockstar has to keep the old money machine humming while building its replacement.

When the new online mode finally arrives, it will have to pull players away from a game they have sunk years into, while also competing with Fortnite and Roblox for attention and spending. Live services are hard, and nothing guarantees a first-try success, even for a studio with this record.
